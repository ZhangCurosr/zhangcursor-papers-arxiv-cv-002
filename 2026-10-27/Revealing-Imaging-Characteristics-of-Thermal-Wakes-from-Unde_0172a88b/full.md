## ABSTRACT

Small uncrewed underwater vehicles are spreading rapidly and threaten maritime security, yet they evade the sonar and magnetic sensors built to track larger submarines. Infrared thermal wakes, generated by hydrodynamic displacement that modulates the cold-skin layer, offer a non-acoustic, non-magnetic alternative even without cooling-water discharge. However, existing studies rely on idealized simulations that fail to capture complex real-world backgrounds, offering limited guidance for target-background discrimination. Here we establish AVP-IR-Wake, the first large-scale, real-world, physically diverse benchmark of 161,485 frames collected over three years. We show that thermal wakes are extended structures composed of discrete vortices, which fundamentally violate the compactness assumption of conventional targets. More importantly, we reveal a fundamental local indistinguishability: individual wake vortices are statistically identical to wind-induced background vortices in the temporal, frequency, and sparse domains, sharing identical low-frequency information and long-range correlations. This directly challenges the prevailing assumptions of compact, transient, and high-frequency targets in conventional detection paradigms. Yet, we establish a global morphological separability: although locally inseparable, the wake as a whole is organized by the vehicle trajectory, whereas background clutter is organized by wind-driven thermal fronts. This intrinsic imaging property explains why representative detectors fail systematically and demonstrates that reliable detection must transition from loca feature extraction to global trajectory-constrained physical modeling. Because the cold-skin layer varies globally and seasonally, forecasting wake observability will require integrating physical oceanography and atmospheric science, linking underwater surveillance directly to the changing ocean surface.

## Introduction

The ocean underpins global ecosystems, trade and maritime security, yet these interests face growing challenges from the rapid proliferation of small uncrewed underwater vehicles<sup>1,</sup> <sup>2</sup>. Inexpensive, quiet and increasingly widespread, these platforms can threaten ports and critical subsea infrastructure while evading sonar and magnetic sensors primarily designed to detect larger submarines<sup>3,</sup> <sup>4</sup>. In 2026, the U.S. Coast Guard acknowledged that conventional approaches cannot reliably detect, track or mitigate these vehicles<sup>5</sup>. Their acoustic signatures can be masked by reverberation in shallow water and at low speeds<sup>6</sup>, while their weak magnetic signatures can be obscured by geomagnetic and environmental noise<sup>7,</sup> <sup>8</sup>. These limitations have prompted increased investment in underwater surveillance, from the UK–US REEF programme to NATO–EU initiatives addressing undersea infrastructure security<sup>1,</sup> <sup>2</sup>. Together, these developments underscore the need for complementary sensing approaches that can reveal underwater vehicles independently of their acoustic and magnetic signatures.

Infrared thermal wake detection offers a non-acoustic, non-magnetic approach to underwater surveillance. Early studies attributed these signatures to cooling-water discharge<sup>9</sup>, but propeller-induced mixing accelerates energy dissipation<sup>10</sup>, modern propulsion systems reduce discharge temperature and volume<sup>11</sup>, and many small uncrewed underwater vehicles release no cooling water at all<sup>12–14</sup>. Recent fluid-physics studies have identified an alternative mechanism<sup>15</sup>: as a vehicle moves, its volumetric displacement transports warmer subsurface water towards the surface<sup>16</sup>, perturbing the cold-skin layer, a submillimetre inversion layer formed by air-sea heat exchange<sup>17</sup>. This process can generate infrared thermal wakes without cooling-water discharge<sup>18</sup>, with morphology and physical characteristics that differ substantially from those of dischargeinduced wakes<sup>9,</sup> <sup>19,</sup> <sup>20</sup>. However, because the cold-skin layer is maintained by natural thermodynamic conditions that are difficult to reproduce in indoor wave tanks, displacement-induced wakes remain challenging to investigate experimentally. Although controlled experiments have shown that surface wake signatures depend strongly on navigation speed, depth and environmental conditions<sup>21</sup>, and numerical studies have modelled wake temperature fields<sup>22,</sup> <sup>23</sup> and simulated their infrared imaging<sup>24</sup>, these approaches cannot fully capture real-world environmental variability. In particular, the environmental conditions governing cold-skin dynamics and wake evolution are difficult to measure and model, leaving the imaging characteristics of displacement induced thermal wakes under realistic and varying conditions insufficiently understood (Supplementary Figure S1).

However, from a detection perspective, merely characterizing the physical properties of a thermal wake is not sufficient. The key challenge is to distinguish the wake from complex natural backgrounds<sup>25,</sup> <sup>26</sup>, whose thermal structures may resemble those of the target. Thus, even if the physical conditions governing wake formation are understood, it remains uncertain whether the resulting infrared signatures can be reliably distinguished from environmental clutter. Absolute signal intensity or isolated thermal anomalies may be insufficient for this purpose because target signatures can overlap with naturally occurring environmental structures<sup>27</sup>. Furthermore, controlled experiments and simulated datasets often involve simplified scenarios<sup>21</sup> that do not adequately represent the diversity of real-world backgrounds, including wind-driven thermal fronts. Even under ideal conditions, the infrared imaging process itself introduces significant discrepancies: due to system nonlinearities, limited camera sensitivity, atmospheric transmission loss, and atmospheric turbulence<sup>28</sup>, the morphological appearance of wake targets in real infrared images can deviate substantially from the idealized physical temperature fields. Targets in controlled experiments are also often constrained to fixed trajectories<sup>21</sup>, limiting their appearance to specific image regions and potentially introducing positional bias. Deep learning models trained on such data may exploit these spatial correlations rather than learn features intrinsic to the thermal wake<sup>29</sup>. Compounding these challenges, acquiring large-scale, real-world detection data is exceptionally difficult. Field trials are resource-intensive and prohibitively expensive, while the sensitive nature of underwater vehicle surveillance often restricts data sharing. Moreover, previous data collection efforts were predominantly physics-driven<sup>30</sup> rather than tailored for computer vision, resulting in a critical scarcity of scene-diverse data required to train robust detectors. These limitations highlight the need to characterize thermal wakes in uncontrolled, physically diverse environments, where their observable properties can be examined alongside the background structures that complicate detection.

However, it remains unclear whether the assumptions underlying conventional detection paradigms hold in complex real-world environments. Conventional infrared dim-target detection typically relies on three assumptions. First, targets are spatially compact<sup>31</sup>. Second, targets are temporally transient or sparse relative to the background<sup>32</sup>. Third, targets occupy a distinct spectral band, typically at higher spatial frequencies than the background<sup>33</sup>. These assumptions have proven effective for detecting small, rigid, hot objects such as aircraft, ships and satellites<sup>34</sup>. Thermal wakes, however, differ fundamentally from such targets. Generated by fluid motion rather than a compact heat source, they can extend over large areas, persist for minutes and comprise multiple discrete vortices (hereafter referred to in an imaging sense as localized thermal anomalies). It therefore remains unclear whether conventional detection assumptions hold for thermal wakes and which imaging properties, if any, can distinguish them from natural background clutter.

To address this gap, we establish AVP-IR-Wake, the first large-scale, real-world benchmark comprising 161,485 infrared frames collected over three years across multiple lakes and vehicle types. We develop a physically grounded annotation protocol that integrates trajectory consistency, hysteresis effects, CFD-assisted simulation and expert cross-validation to localize weak and ambiguous wakes. Using this dataset, we find that individual wake vortices are statistically indistinguishable from wind-induced background vortices in the temporal, frequency and sparse domains. This local indistinguishability challenges the assumptions underlying conventional detection paradigms. Yet, at the global scale, the wake retains a distinct morphological organization constrained by the vehicle trajectory, whereas background clutter is organized by wind-driven thermal fronts. Evaluations of representative detectors further show that conventional detection priors, including compactness, high-frequency content, sparsity and temporal profiles, are insufficient to reliably identify these wakes. Together, these findings point to the need for detection approaches that move beyond local feature extraction towards global, trajectory-constrained physical modeling. Because wake observability depends on the coupled ocean-atmosphere boundary layer, which varies across regions and seasons, understanding thermal wakes may also provide insights into how environmental conditions shape underwater vehicle observability and stealth.

## Results

## Statistical Characterization of AVP-IR-Wake

The AVP-IR-Wake dataset is the first large-scale, real-world, physically diverse benchmark for thermal-wake detection. It comprises 161,485 infrared frames collected over three years across multiple lakes, primarily at Tianjin Port (adjacent to the sea), using a cooled infrared camera with a thermal sensitivity of 20 mK and covering multiple underwater vehicle types (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2). The dataset captures the extreme conditions under which thermal wakes must be detected. Signal-to-noise ratios range from −59.18 to 4.99 dB, with a median of −4.09 dB, and 93.4% of targets fall below 0 dB, placing the majority of the dataset in the regime of extremely weak signals. Aspect ratios range from 0.159 to 11.492, with a median of 4.497; 9.9% are below 1 and 90.0% are above 1, reflecting the elongated geometry of the wake. Target areas span 124.4 to 270,091.4 pixels<sup>2</sup>, with a median of 48,838.7 pixels<sup>2</sup> and 5th, 25th, 75th, and 95th percentiles of 2,902.5, 21,370.2, 84,585.4, and 140,471.0 pixels<sup>2</sup>, confirming that the wake occupies a large, spatially distributed region rather than a compact object. Target positions show a central bias because the camera orientation was adjusted during acquisition to place most targets near the image center. Background complexity, quantified by variance-weighted information entropy (VIE)<sup>35</sup>, has a median of 16,137.4 and a range of 7,467.2 to 22,945.8, with 25th and 75th percentiles of 14,562.1 and 19,080.9, placing most sequences in the high or very high complexity regime (definitions are provided in Supplementary Section S2). Environmental diversity is built into the acquisition protocol. Wind speeds span Beaufort 2 to 6, because wind accelerates target dissipation and modulates the cold-skin layer. The data include summer, autumn, and winter in an approximate ratio of 4:4:1, reflecting seasonal changes in cold-skin properties. The ratio of ROV to AUV data is approximately 1:9. Because cold-skin properties also differ between day and night, the data were collected at eight time points throughout the day, with the temporal distribution split between morning (0:00, 2:00, 4:00 6:00, 8:00) and afternoon (14:00, 16:00, 18:00) at a ratio of 1:2 and uniform sampling within each period. Together, these characteristics establish AVP-IR-Wake as a benchmark that probes the limits of infrared detection: the targets are extremely weak, spatially extended, highly elongated, embedded in complex and variable backgrounds, and observed under a wide range of environmental conditions.

Together, these observations establish that the target and background separability assumption fails for thermal wakes: the environment does not merely add noise, but generates structures that are structurally indistinguishable from the target.

Thermal wakes cannot be separated from background clutter in the frequency domain, in either single-frame or multi-frame settings. We first examined whether spatial-frequency analysis could isolate the wake from the background. Multiband decomposition of wake images shows that the wake energy is concentrated in the low-frequency subband (L2\_LL), while all high-frequency subbands contain only background texture and noise. Background regions exhibit the same lowfrequency dominance, so no spatial-frequency band separates wake from clutter (Figure 4a). This behaviour follows directly from physics: thermal diffusion acts as a low-pass filter on the surface temperature field, suppressing high-frequency components regardless of their origin. We then extended the analysis to the spatiotemporal domain using a retina-inspired filter that responds to frame-to-frame intensity changes. If the wake possessed a distinct temporal-frequency signature, the filter would respond to it; it does not. The filter produces no distinguishable response in wake sequences, whereas it responds strongly to satellite small targets and gaseous objects that possess transient motion (Figure 4b). The wake therefore also lacks a distinctive temporal frequency signature. Together, these results show that the wake and background clutter occupy the same low-frequency region in both the spatial and spatiotemporal domains, so frequency-domain detectors cannot separate the two. This violates the high-frequency assumption commonly adopted in dim-target and moving-target detection, which presupposes a distinct frequency band for the target.

Because the global trajectory structure is the key to target-background separability, the entire target region was delineated as the bounding box following expert recommendations. This inevitably results in background clutter occupying the dominant spatial proportion within these target boxes. To quantify this, we extracted UMAP<sup>41</sup> embeddings of 130,000 randomly sampled target boxes and corresponding background regions from the same images. The two classes are completely inseparable in the embedding space (Supplementary Fig. S7), quantitatively confirming that the visual features within the target boxes are overwhelmingly governed by the surrounding background clutter.

The wake remains globally separable from background clutter in its morphology, even though the two are locally indistinguishable. The preceding analyses show that individual wake vortices cannot be separated from background vortices in either the temporal or the frequency domain. This raises the question of whether any imaging property distinguishes the wake from the background. Figure 5a shows that one does, at the global scale. Although the constituent vortices of the wake are not individually distinguishable from background vortices, the overall extension of the wake follows the physical trajectory of the vehicle (red arrows), yielding a distinctive global morphology that background clutter does not share. A detailed physical analysis is provided in Supplementary Section S5. This trajectory constraint arises because the surface thermal perturbation accumulates along the vehicle path: the source term is localized around the moving vehicle, and the resulting surface anomaly is the integral of contributions from the vehicle’s past positions. The global morphology therefore reflects the trajectory, regardless of local vortex dynamics. Figure 5b further shows that, because underwater vehicles rarely travel at constant speed along a straight line, the constituent vortices appear stochastically in time and space with non-fixed spacing, making the local structure inherently unpredictable. Background vortices exhibit a different global organization. Figure 5c shows that wind forcing generates thermal fronts, and background vortices evolve along these fronts<sup>42</sup>, forming a characteristic global structure determined by the wind field rather than by any vehicle trajectory. Figure 5d shows that gusts weaken the cold-skin temperature gradient, reducing both target and background vortex intensities. However, because the ocean is thermally stratified, this reduction is depth-dependent. At certain submergence depths, the vehicle’s volumetric displacement can still transport warmer subsurface water across the stratified layers to the surface, producing a detectable anomaly even when the ambient cold-skin gradient is weak. In contrast, background vortices generated by wind forcing are confined to the surface layer and are therefore suppressed more strongly. As a result, there exists a depth window in which the target remains observable despite the overall reduction in signal strength. The two classes of structures therefore differ in their global organizing principle: the wake is organized by the vehicle trajectory, whereas background vortices are organized by wind-driven thermal fronts. This global morphological separability is an intrinsic imaging property of thermal wakes. It explains why the wake can be visually distinguished from clutter at large scales, despite being statistically indistinguishable from it at small scales. It also indicates that detection methods operating only on local features cannot exploit this property, because the distinguishing information lies in the global organization of the wake rather than in the local statistics of individual vortices.

## Quantifying the Breakdown of Conventional Detection Assumptions

Directly quantifying how thermal wakes deviate from conventional target assumptions is challenging because infrared wake images are high-dimensional signals shaped by coupled hydrodynamic and environmental processes. Rather than measuring deviations from predefined image statistics, we use existing detection algorithms as quantitative probes of the assumptions embedded in their designs. Algorithmic performance reflects not only model capacity, but also the compatibility between inductive biases and the underlying physical characteristics of the observed targets.

We evaluated a diverse set of detectors, each configured according to the recommendations in its original publication, and grouped them by the priors they encode. General-purpose single-frame detectors make minimal assumptions about target appearance, while general-purpose multi-frame detectors assume that temporal information aids detection. Infrared dim-target single-frame detectors encode either high-frequency or low-rank/sparse priors, and infrared dim-target multi-frame detectors encode high-frequency, low-rank/sparse, or temporal-profile priors. Because of memory constraints, most multi frame detectors capture only short-term spatiotemporal relationships spanning about five frames; DeepPro is the exception, processing up to 100 frames by encoding only the temporal profile. Table 1 summarizes the results. Across all categories, average precision remains at or near zero, and several methods fail to converge (Supplementary Section S4 presents the loss curves of representative detectors. Note that our dataset split was performed following the recommendations of practitioners, and variations in conditions have a substantial impact on both target and background characteristics. Consequently, once a detector fails to capture appropriate representations, its test-set performance generalizes poorly). This systematic failure is not attributable to model capacity or training strategy; rather, it provides a quantitative measure of the mismatch between conventional inductive biases and the physical structure of thermal wakes. Notably, methods that explicitly model low-rank and sparse priors, including LRRNet<sup>32</sup>, RPCANet<sup>++56</sup>, and TenRPCANet<sup>74</sup>, as well as DeepPro<sup>76</sup>, which is designed to capture temporal-profile characteristics, all fail to converge. This is consistent with our analyses showing that the low-rank and sparse decomposition assumption is invalidated by the long-range temporal correlations of the wake, and that temporal profiles are locally indistinguishable. CHAL<sup>75</sup> converges but achieves near-chance performance, because its causal-invariance assumption, which treats target characteristics as independent of the environment, contradicts the environment-dependent nature of thermal wakes, whose physical manifestation evolves together with the surrounding conditions. Furthermore, VSF RCNN<sup>40</sup>, a detector designed for gaseous objects, targets highly non-rigid plumes, but its encoded Gaussian dispersion prior fundamentally differs from the physical characteristics of thermal wakes, particularly when the wake consists of discrete vortices, resulting in similarly near-zero performance. Notably, single-frame detectors generally outperform multi-frame detectors; because the spatiotemporal characteristics of thermal wakes are highly complex, and the wake exhibits stochastic local occurrences and long-range temporal correlations, existing multi-frame methods have a fundamental gap between their temporal exploitation strategies (e.g., short-term optical flow or frame differencing) and the actual wake properties, rendering their temporal modeling ineffective. Ultimately, none of the encoded priors, including compactness, high-frequency content, sparsity, temporal profile, and Gaussian dispersion, carries information about target presence in thermal wakes.

## Discussion

## Toward Detectors Matched to Thermal Wake Physics

The universal failure in Table 1 is not a call for larger models or longer training, but for a different class of detector. Current architectures encode priors such as local compactness, high-frequency content, sparsity, and temporal profiles, all incompatible with the physical structure of thermal wakes. A detector suited to this task must instead encode local indistinguishability, stochastic vortex occurrence, low-frequency energy concentration, long-range temporal correlation, and global trajectoryconstrained morphology, and must represent the background as wind-organized thermal fronts rather than random noise. This reframes detection from local window classification to global state estimation under physical constraints: the detector tests whether a spatially extended structure consistent with a vehicle trajectory is present, while allowing vortices to appear at random positions and times. Adding global attention to existing architectures is unlikely to suffice, because the required inductive bias is a physical constraint on target organization. Moreover, since background vortices are often associated with wind-driven thermal fronts and wind fields are routinely measured<sup>77,</sup> <sup>78</sup>, future detectors could incorporate environmental metadata to achieve robust detection beyond imagery alone<sup>79</sup>.

## Implications for Underwater Vehicle Stealth

Whereas previous stealth research has focused on controlling cooling-water discharge, a controllable design parameter, our observations demonstrate that thermal wake detectability is governed by the coupled state of the ocean–atmosphere boundary layer. Local indistinguishability implies that reducing the intensity of individual vortices cannot alter their statistical equivalence to background vortices, making vortex-scale suppression ineffective. Global morphological separability implies that the wake follows the vehicle trajectory while background clutter is organized by wind-driven thermal fronts. Because nocturnal cooling stabilizes the cold-skin layer and daytime solar heating weakens it<sup>80</sup>, identical operating conditions are more detectable at night. Because gusts suppress surface-confined background vortices more strongly than subsurface-originating wake signatures, the depth–stealth tradeoff inverts with wind speed: deeper operation is preferable in calm conditions, whereas shallow operation can be safer under strong wind. Stealth is therefore not a fixed capability that can be designed into a platform but a state-dependent property of the ocean–atmosphere system, requiring real-time environmental assessment rather than static design.

## Underwater Vehicle Observability as An Emergent Property of The Coupled Ocean–Atmosphere System

Underwater vehicle observability is governed not only by vehicle-induced hydrodynamic disturbances, but also by the dynamic state of the ocean–atmosphere boundary layer through which these disturbances are transferred into observable signals<sup>81–83</sup>. Similar to wave breaking<sup>84</sup>, where mechanical energy dissipation modifies the ocean surface skin and regulates exchanges of heat, momentum, and gases between the ocean and atmosphere<sup>85</sup>, a submerged vehicle acts as a localized mechanical forcing that perturbs this coupled interface. The resulting infrared wake is therefore not a direct thermal imprint of the vehicle, but an emergent signature shaped by turbulent transport, cold-skin modulation, surface heat exchange, and atmospheric forcing<sup>86,</sup> <sup>87</sup>. Our observations demonstrate that environmental coupling does not simply attenuate wake signatures; it reorganizes the information available for observation, obscuring local thermal structures and spectral characteristics while preserving trajectory constrained global morphology associated with vehicle motion. Consequently, wake observability should be considered a spatiotemporally varying property of the ocean–atmosphere system rather than an intrinsic attribute of the vehicle itself<sup>88</sup>. Because the thermal state of the upper ocean varies across geographic regions, seasons, diurnal cycles, and meteorological regimes<sup>89–91</sup>, the detectability of underwater vehicles is expected to vary accordingly. Future changes in ocean warming, upper-ocean stratification, atmospheric circulation, and extreme weather patterns may further modify these boundary-layer conditions<sup>92–94</sup>, introducing new challenges for predicting underwater vehicle observability in a changing climate. Addressing these challenges will require interdisciplinary frameworks that couple underwater vehicle hydrodynamics, upper-ocean turbulence, atmospheric forcing, and remote sensing. Oceanographers will need to determine how regional hydrographic states, stratification, and submesoscale processes regulate the persistence of vehicle-induced disturbances; atmospheric scientists will need to quantify how evolving wind regimes, radiative forcing, and air–sea heat exchange modify the thermal transfer pathway; remote sensing communities will need observations capable of resolving fine-scale thermal skin variability across diverse ocean states<sup>95</sup>; and computer vision researchers will need physically informed representations that capture evolving global structures rather than relying solely on local target appearance. Such advances will not only improve assessments of underwater vehicle detectability and concealment, but also provide broader insights into how localized mechanical disturbances are transformed into remotely sensed signatures within a changing ocean–atmosphere system.

## Limitations and Future Work

This study focuses on revealing the general structural characteristics and observability patterns of thermal wakes under realistic environmental variability, rather than establishing environment-specific quantitative parameterizations of wake intensity or morphology. Such parameterizations require precise measurements of coupled oceanic, atmospheric, and hydrodynamic variables, which are difficult to obtain in natural environments without sacrificing the environmental diversity essential for identifying general wake characteristics. Moreover, the factors governing wake manifestation vary across regions, seasons, and meteorological conditions, and quantitative relationships derived from limited controlled settings may introduce systematic biases. Therefore, this work emphasizes robust structural representations that remain observable across diverse conditions, providing a foundation for future predictive models of wake observability. The structural characteristics identified here are derived from a physically diverse dataset collected over three years across multiple lakes, environmental conditions, and vehicle platforms. The consistency of these patterns suggests that wake organization is governed primarily by the underlying cold-skin modulation mechanism and its interaction with the environment, rather than by specific vehicle configurations. Future observations across broader oceanic regimes will be required to establish predictive relationships between ocean–atmosphere states and thermal wake manifestation. Beyond detection, the trajectory-constrained morphology revealed here may enable inverse estimation of vehicle depth, speed, and motion history, transforming thermal wake analysis from binary identification toward quantitative understanding of vehicle–ocean–atmosphere interactions.

## Method

## AVP-IR-Wake Dataset Collection

Operating Envelope Analysis. A thermal wake is produced when hydrodynamic displacement modulates the cold-skin layer, so not every depth–speed combination gives a detectable wake. To estimate the maximum detectable dive depth, we used the original hydrodynamic model<sup>15,</sup> <sup>18,</sup> <sup>22,</sup> <sup>23</sup> with the same parameters and settings (we thank Assoc. Prof. Yongcheng Du, who developed this model, for his help), together with the vehicle CAD model, its motion profile, and the camera’s 20 mK sensitivity. Under these conditions, the maximum detectable dive depth was 4.5D, where D is the vehicle diameter.

Data Collection. To ensure sufficient physical and environmental diversity, we conducted extensive field experiments from 2023 to 2025 across multiple inland lakes, primarily at Tianjin Port, as well as inland seas near Zhuhai and Shenzhen, China, using several types of underwater vehicles under diverse operating and environmental conditions. Tianjin Port was selected as the primary site because it more closely approximates the port surveillance scenario for underwater vehicles. In the experiments, we primarily employed two types of underwater vehicles: remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) and autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs), which have distinctly different configurations. Among the AUVs, we used the Orange Shark II-T and the Black Shark I-A from Deepinfar. Because these vehicles operate fully submerged, a loss of control would result in sinking. For safety, each vehicle is equipped with an automatic ascent safeguard that triggers upon system failure or upon reaching a preset depth limit. This limit, however, was lower than our calculated 4.5D threshold. We therefore worked with Deepinfar’s R&D team to modify the vehicles and reconfigure their controller parameters accordingly. Vehicle specifications were obtained from the manufacturer’s published data (Deepinfar, https://www.deepinfar.com/Cn/About/index.html).

## Data Annotation and Splitting

Data Annotation. To annotate the infrared thermal wakes, we developed a physically grounded protocol that integrates multiple complementary priors. The overall wake morphology is self-similar to the vehicle trajectory: straight-line motion produces a straight band, turns produce a curved wake, and hovering collapses the wake into an isolated vortex, while the internal structure remains independent of trajectory shape (Supplementary Section S5). Wake formation also exhibits a temporal lag, because the volumetric effect requires time to transport subsurface water to the cold-skin layer; this lag increases with depth and decreases with speed, and by recording camera timestamps and vehicle passage times it can be used to reject background false alarms. We further performed CFD simulations to predict the expected image morphology under each depth and speed condition ((Further results are provided in Supplementary Figure S9.)). These predictions guided annotation under most conditions, but when gust-induced thermal fronts aligned with the vehicle motion, the false front closely resembled a real wake, and such cases were resolved through multi-expert cross-validation rather than CFD alone. Because the vehicle trajectory and camera position were logged, the relative position between the camera and the wake provided an auxiliary cue for restricting the search region (Supplementary Section S6). Based on these priors, a panel of experts performed independent annotation and cross-validation, with ambiguous cases reviewed by researchers specializing in thermal-wake fluid dynamics. A gold-standard subset was annotated by experts, and multiple annotators cross-annotated and validated against it, using IoU thresholds of 0.9 for strip-like wakes and 0.8 for vortical targets to reflect their stochastic and discontinuous nature. Annotations meeting these criteria were voted to select the final bounding box, and challenging samples were further reviewed. No videos were excluded on the basis of annotation difficulty or low signal strength.

Data Splitting. Data splitting was performed at the acquisition-day level to prevent data leakage and evaluate generalization to previously unseen operating conditions. Of the 252 collected videos, 52 were excluded (20 for CFD validation and parameter optimization, 32 as redundant or simple), and the remaining 200 videos were split by acquisition-day into training and test sets at an about 3:2 ratio. Notably, the lengths of these video sequences vary. This is because we removed highly similar, redundant frames that occurred under the same operating condition during preprocessing. This redundancy filtering ensures that the dataset captures meaningful temporal dynamics rather than repetitive static states. This design reflects the practical scenario in which a detector must operate across continuously varying environmental states rather than interpolate within a fixed acquisition domain. When learned representations are aligned with the underlying physical mechanisms governing target formation, generalization across such variations is expected to emerge naturally<sup>40,</sup> <sup>96,</sup> <sup>97</sup>. However, if models rely on environment-specific appearances or inappropriate assumptions about target characteristics, their performance will inevitably degrade when transferred to unseen conditions. Because depth, speed, weather, and wind conditions can vary substantially between acquisition days, this split provides a stringent assessment of whether existing detection paradigms capture physically meaningful and transferable representations or exploit incidental environmental cues (Further results are provided in Supplementary Figure S10. An FID score<sup>98</sup> of 252.467 further indicates the substantial distribution discrepancy between the training and test sets, as well as the environment-dependent nature of the targets, with detailed analyses provided in Supplementary Section S7.). The proportions of continuous stripe-like targets and discrete vortex-cluster wakes are consistent between the training and test sets, helping ensure that the evaluation is not confounded by differences in the prevalence of these two major wake morphologies.

## Frequency-Domain Analysis

Multiband Decomposition. Spatial-frequency separability was assessed using multiband decomposition based on the two dimensional discrete wavelet transform. Each infrared frame was converted to grayscale and decomposed using the Haar wavelet with two decomposition levels and symmetric boundary extension. This produced seven subbands: L1\_HH, L1\_HL L1\_LH, L2\_HH, L2\_HL, L2\_LH, and L2\_LL. The Haar basis was chosen because it provides compact spatial support and is well suited to detecting localized discontinuities without introducing ringing artifacts, which is important for identifying weak and spatially confined features in infrared imagery. Symmetric extension was used to avoid boundary discontinuities that could contaminate the high-frequency subbands. For each subband, the energy was computed as the sum of squared wavelet coefficients, and the energy fraction was defined as the ratio of that subband’s energy to the total energy summed over all subbands. This normalization ensures that the energy fraction is independent of the overall image intensity and directly reflects how the signal is distributed across spatial frequencies. The same decomposition and energy computation were applied to background regions, which were selected as areas of the same size as the target region but located away from the wake. To verify that the observed low-frequency dominance was not an artifact of the specific wavelet basis or decomposition depth, we repeated the analysis with the Daubechies db2 wavelet and with three decomposition levels. The qualitative result remained unchanged: the wake energy was concentrated in the lowest-frequency subband (L2\_LL or its equivalent), and no spatial-frequency band separated the wake from background clutter.

Retina-Inspired Spatiotemporal Filtering. Retina-inspired filters are advantageous for characterizing spatiotemporal signatures because they emulate the magnocellular pathway’s selective sensitivity to motion and transient intensity changes<sup>99</sup>. Spatiotemporal separability was assessed using a retina-inspired cellular automaton<sup>100</sup> that models the magnocellular pathway of motion processing. The filter operates on consecutive grayscale frames and consists of five layers. The first layer applies photoreceptor adaptation: values above a threshold $\theta _ { p } = 0 . 1$ are transformed by $g _ { p } \operatorname { t a n h } ( I - \theta _ { p } )$ with $g _ { p } = 1 . 5$ , while values below are scaled by 0.1. The second layer models horizontal cells as $S _ { h } = \operatorname* { m a x } ( S _ { p } - \sigma _ { h } ( S _ { p } * K _ { h } ) , 0 )$ , where $K _ { h }$ is a $3 \times 3$ Gaussian kernel with standard deviation $1 . 0 , \sigma _ { h } = 0 . 3$ , and ∗ denotes convolution. The third layer models bipolar cells with ON and OFF pathways as $C _ { t } = \operatorname* { m a x } ( g _ { b } ( S _ { h } - \theta _ { b } ) , 0 ) + \operatorname* { m a x } ( g _ { b } ( - S _ { h } - \theta _ { b } ) , 0 )$ , with $\begin{array} { r } { \theta _ { b } = 0 . 2 \ \mathrm { { a n d } } \ g _ { b } = 2 . 0 . } \end{array}$ The fourth layer models amacrine cells, which accumulate temporal changes. For the first frame, $R _ { t } = \beta \sqrt { ( \nabla _ { x } C _ { t } ) ^ { 2 } + ( \nabla _ { y } C _ { t } ) ^ { 2 } }$ ; for subsequent frames, $R _ { t } = \beta | C _ { t } - S _ { \mathrm { p r e v } , b } |$ , with $\beta = 1 . 2$ . The accumulated signal is $S _ { a } = \alpha S _ { \mathrm { p r e v } , a } + ( 1 - \alpha ) R _ { t }$ with $\alpha = 0 . 8$ . The fifth layer models magnocellular ganglion cells as $S _ { m } = g _ { m } \operatorname* { m a x } ( \operatorname { t a n h } ( M _ { s } + M _ { r } - \theta _ { m } ) , 0 )$ , where $M _ { s } = I _ { t } * K _ { m } , M _ { r } = \gamma _ { r } S _ { a } , I _ { t } = C _ { t } + \gamma _ { a } S _ { a } ,$ with $\gamma _ { a } = 0 . 5 , \gamma _ { r } = 0 . 7 , g _ { m } = 2 . 5 , \theta _ { m } = 0 . 3 , \mathrm { a n d } K _ { m } \mathrm { ~ a ~ } 5 \times 5$ Mexican-hat kernel with $\sigma _ { 1 } = 1 . 0 , \sigma _ { 2 } = 2 . 0$ , and surround weight 0.5. The final output is $M _ { t } = \mathrm { E n h a n c e } ( \eta _ { m } S _ { m } + ( 1 - \eta _ { m } ) S _ { a } )$ , where $\eta _ { m } = 0 . 7$ and Enhance applies gamma correction with $\gamma _ { p } = 0 . 8$ , followed by bilateral filtering and normalization to [0,255]. The same filter and parameters were applied to thermal wake sequences from AVP-IR-Wake, satellite small-target sequences from IRSatVideo-LEO, and gaseous object sequences from GOD-Video. All sequences were converted to grayscale and processed frame by frame. A response was considered distinguishable if the peak amplitude of M<sub>t</sub> exceeded three times the standard deviation of the background response.

# Revealing Imaging Characteristics of Thermal Wakes from Underwater Vehicles Without Cooling-Water Discharge

## S1 Comparison with Simulated Imaging

Synthetic infrared imaging has been widely used to study thermal wakes, typically relying on two distinct strategies: direct computational simulation and target embedding. Neither strategy can substitute for real-world observations when evaluating detection algorithms.

The first strategy generates images by converting Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) temperature fields directly into radiance fields. While this approach accurately reproduces the idealized physical morphology of the wake, it fails to capture the complex environmental coupling, as shown in Figure S1. Real ocean surfaces are continuously modulated by wind, waves, and solar radiation, generating thermal fronts and background clutter. Since the fundamental challenge of target detection lies in separating the target from this complex background, computationally simulated images lack the realistic environmental interference necessary to evaluate a detector’s true discriminative capability.

The second strategy attempts to preserve realistic backgrounds by embedding synthetic wake signatures into real infrared scenes. However, this approach violates physical consistency. In reality, the background state (e.g., wind speed, wave roughness, and cold-skin temperature gradient) is physically coupled with the wake generation process. A wake observed under strong wind conditions possesses different morphological and thermal characteristics than one generated under calm seas. Directly overlaying a wake patch onto an arbitrary background ignores this coupling. Furthermore, this embedding approach is constrained by the lack of high-sensitivity, long-term infrared observations of the ocean surface required to serve as high-fidelit background templates.

Consequently, neither simulation strategy provides a valid proxy for algorithm benchmarking. The idealized targets of computational simulations and the physically inconsistent backgrounds of embedded datasets fail to reflect the local indistinguishability between wakes and natural clutter observed in our real-world dataset. This underscores why AVP-IR-Wake, acquired entirely through high-sensitivity field observations, is indispensable for characterizing the true imaging properties of thermal wakes.

## S2 Metrics

## S2.1 Dataset Metrics

The difficulty and physical diversity of AVP-IR-Wake are mainly characterized by two metrics: signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and variance-weighted information entropy (VIE). SNR is defined as

$$
\mathrm { S N R } = 1 0 \log \left( \frac { | \mu _ { t } - \mu _ { b } | } { \sigma _ { b } } \right) ,\tag{1}
$$

where $\mu _ { t }$ denotes the mean intensity of the target region, and $\mu _ { b }$ and $\sigma _ { b }$ denote the mean and standard deviation of the local background region, respectively.

For the t-th frame $\mathbf { I } _ { t }$ in a sequence, its VIE is defined as

$$
\mathrm { V I E } = - \sum _ { \beta = 0 } ^ { 2 5 5 } \left( \beta - \bar { \beta } _ { t } \right) ^ { 2 } p ( \mathbf { I } _ { t } = \beta ) \log p ( \mathbf { I } _ { t } = \beta ) ,\tag{2}
$$

where $\beta$ denotes the grayscale variable in the histogram, $p ( \mathbf { I } _ { t } = \beta )$ is the probability of grayscale value $\beta$ occurring in frame $\mathbf { I } _ { t } ,$ and $\hat { \beta } _ { t }$ is the mean grayscale value of $\mathbf { I } _ { t } .$ . The VIE metric thus quantifies the fluctuation of the grayscale distribution. Based on the sequence’s average VIE, the background complexity is divided into four levels: low (0–1,000), medium (1,000–5,000), high (5,000–10,000), and very high (>10,000).

## S2.2 Evaluation Metrics

We report standard COCO-style detection metrics: AP, $\mathrm { A P } _ { 5 0 } , \mathrm { A P } _ { 7 5 } , \mathrm { A P } _ { s } , \mathrm { A P } _ { m } , \mathrm { A P } _ { l }$ , and AR. AP is the average precision, computed as the area under the precision–recall curve, averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of $0 . 0 5 . \mathrm { A P } _ { 5 0 }$ and $\mathsf { A P 7 5 }$ are the AP values at IoU thresholds of 0.50 and 0.75, respectively. $\mathbf { A P } _ { s } , \mathbf { A P } _ { m } ,$ , and $\mathsf { A P } _ { l }$ denote AP for small, medium, and large objects, defined by object area following the COCO convention: small $( \mathrm { a r e a } < 3 2 ^ { 2 }$ pixels), medium $( 3 2 ^ { 2 } \leq$ area $\leq 9 6 ^ { 2 }$ pixels), and large (area $> 9 6 ^ { 2 }$ pixels). ## S3 Temporal correlation analysis

For each point of interest, we extract the pixel intensity time series $x _ { t }$ from the infrared image sequence, where $t = 1 , \ldots , N$ The temporal mean is denoted by $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { x } = N ^ { - 1 } \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } x _ { t } } \end{array}$ . The autocorrelation function $\rho ( l )$ at lag $l \left( l = 0 , 1 , \ldots , L \right)$ is defined as

$$
\rho ( l ) = \frac { \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N - l } ( x _ { t } - \bar { x } ) \big ( x _ { t + l } - \bar { x } \big ) } { \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } \big ( x _ { t } - \bar { x } \big ) ^ { 2 } } ,\tag{3}
$$

with $\rho ( 0 ) = 1$ . For two time series x<sub>t</sub> and y<sub>t</sub>, the cross-correlation function $\rho _ { x y } ( l )$ at lag $l \left( l = - L , \ldots , L \right)$ is defined as

$$
\rho _ { x y } ( l ) = \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { \frac { \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N - l } ( x _ { t } - \bar { x } ) \big ( y _ { t + l } - \bar { y } \big ) } { \sqrt { \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } ( x _ { t } - \bar { x } ) ^ { 2 } \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } ( y _ { t } - \bar { y } ) ^ { 2 } } } , } & { l > 0 , } \\ { \frac { \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } ( x _ { t } - \bar { x } ) \big ( y _ { t } - \bar { y } \big ) } { \sqrt { \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } ( x _ { t } - \bar { x } ) ^ { 2 } \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } ( y _ { t } - \bar { y } ) ^ { 2 } } } , } & { l = 0 , } \\ { \frac { \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N + l } ( x _ { t - l } - \bar { x } ) \big ( y _ { t } - \bar { y } \big ) } { \sqrt { \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } ( x _ { t } - \bar { x } ) ^ { 2 } \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { N } ( y _ { t } - \bar { y } ) ^ { 2 } } } , } & { l < 0 . } \end{array} \right.\tag{4}
$$

To summarize pairwise correlations, we construct a positive-correlation matrix $M ^ { + }$ and a negative-correlation matrix $M ^ { - }$ , with elements

$$
M _ { i j } ^ { + } = \operatorname* { m a x } _ { l > 0 } \rho _ { x _ { i } x _ { j } } ( l ) , \qquad M _ { i j } ^ { - } = \operatorname* { m i n } _ { l < 0 } \rho _ { x _ { i } x _ { j } } ( l ) ,\tag{5}
$$

where $x _ { i }$ and $x _ { j }$ denote the time series at points i and $j ,$ respectively. Here L is the maximum lag considered, set to $L = 5 0 0$ in our analysis. All time series are mean-subtracted prior to correlation analysis.

## S5 Physical Basis of Trajectory–Morphology Consistency

The trajectory–morphology consistency observed in the main text can be understood from the governing equation of the surface temperature field. Let $T ( \mathbf { x } , t )$ denote the temperature of the cold-skin layer on the free surface, U(x,t) the surface velocity field, and κ the thermal diffusivity of water. The evolution of the surface temperature perturbation $\theta = T - T _ { 0 }$ relative to the background field $T _ { 0 }$ is governed by the advection–diffusion equation

$$
\frac { \partial \theta } { \partial t } + \mathbf { U } \cdot \nabla \theta = \kappa \nabla ^ { 2 } \theta + S ( \mathbf { x } - \mathbf { r } _ { \nu } ( t ) ) ,\tag{6}
$$

where $\mathbf { r } _ { \nu } ( t )$ is the instantaneous horizontal position of the vehicle along its trajectory, and S is the source term representing the local thermal perturbation induced by volumetric displacement. The source term is spatially localized around the vehicle because the upward transport of subsurface water occurs in the immediate vicinity of the hull and the propeller wake. Because the perturbation is weak, the nonlinear advection term can be approximated by a known surface flow. The formal solution of Eq. (6) can then be written as a convolution with the Green’s function G of the advection–diffusion operator,

$$
\theta ( \mathbf { x } , t ) = \int _ { - \infty } ^ { t } \int _ { \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 } } G ( \mathbf { x } - \mathbf { x } ^ { \prime } , t - t ^ { \prime } ) S ( \mathbf { x } ^ { \prime } - \mathbf { r } _ { \nu } ( t ^ { \prime } ) ) \mathrm { d } \mathbf { x } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { d } t ^ { \prime } .\tag{7}
$$

Since the Green’s function is spatially localized, the surface temperature anomaly at a given time is the superposition of contributions from the vehicle’s past positions along its trajectory. In the limit where the thermal relaxation time is long

## S6 Geometric Basis of Camera-to-Wake Relative Positioning

The relative position between the camera and the wake can be approximated from the vehicle trajectory and the known camera pose. Let the horizontal position of the vehicle at time t be ${ \bf p } _ { \nu } ( t ) = ( x _ { \nu } ( t ) , y _ { \nu } ( t ) )$ , and let the camera be located at $\mathbf { p } _ { c } = ( x _ { c } , y _ { c } , h _ { c } )$ , where $h _ { c }$ is the camera altitude. $$

## S7 Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) Analysis

To quantitatively evaluate the distribution shift between the training and test sets, we compute the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID). Because our dataset is split at the acquisition-day level, the training and test sets contain entirely different environmental conditions (e.g., wind speed, solar radiation, and cold-skin state). Standard pixel-level comparisons fail to capture the structural and semantic differences induced by these environmental changes. FID, in contrast, measures the distance between the high-level feature distributions extracted by a pre-trained network, providing a robust metric for domain discrepancy.

We extract feature representations from all frames using a pre-trained Inception-v3 network. The feature vectors are then modeled as two multivariate Gaussian distributions: $\mathcal { N } ( \mu _ { t } , \Sigma _ { t } )$ for the training set and $\mathcal { N } ( \mu _ { \nu } , \Sigma _ { \nu } )$ for the test set. The FID is computed as the Fréchet distance between these two distributions:

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \mathrm { F I D } = \Vert \mu _ { t } - \mu _ { \nu } \Vert ^ { 2 } + \mathrm { T r } \left( \Sigma _ { t } + \Sigma _ { \nu } - 2 ( \Sigma _ { t } \Sigma _ { \nu } ) ^ { 1 / 2 } \right) . } \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

The first term measures the distance between the feature means, while the second term captures the difference in feature covariances. For our dataset, the computed FID is 252.467. To contextualize this value, FID scores for typical independent and identically distributed (IID) splits of standard vision datasets are usually in the single or low double digits. A score of 252.467 signifies a profound distribution discrepancy. This large distance is not driven by sensor noise or annotation artifacts, but rather by the substantial simultaneous shifts in both target and background characteristics under different environmental conditions. For instance, a wake observed under calm nighttime conditions (deep, continuous stripe) and a wake observed under strong daytime winds (shallow, fragmented vortex clusters) map to vastly different regions in the feature space. This quantitative result directly corroborates the environment-dependent physical nature of thermal wakes, and underscores why detectors relying on local, environment-specific cues fail to generalize from the training set to the unseen test set.
